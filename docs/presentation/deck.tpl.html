A grassroots organisation that can physically reach these places

Blue Lagos Sustainability Initiative works directly inside the riverine and coastal communities documented in this report — building the relationships, the field survey infrastructure and the named local contacts that make a register like this possible at all.

The mandate is simple: no community is left behind, regardless of how difficult it is to physically reach. Many of these settlements are accessible only by boat, and roughly nine in ten have never had a government or NGO programme of any kind.

Part 01/IntroductionBaseline report, Part 1

These communities are not under-served by accident. They are under-recorded.

Riverine and coastal settlements sit outside the datasets Lagos State plans with. Without a register, they cannot be budgeted for, sequenced, or monitored — so they are skipped, and the skipping is invisible.

Between 2025 and 2026 we ran a door-to-door baseline of 134 communities across 7 Local Government Areas, so that every subsequent conversation with a government partner starts from the same verified, community-level evidence rather than estimates.

Part 02/The dataBaseline report, Part 2

How the evidence was built

  1. Built outward from real relationshipsCoverage started in Epe and Ibeju-Lekki, where Blue Lagos already worked, then extended systematically across Ojo, Amuwo Odofin, Badagry, Eti Osa and Apapa.
  2. A working definition of a communityA named cluster with its own recognised head or ruler, an identifiable population, and a physical location separate from neighbouring clusters.
  3. Interviewed through the head, not around himEnumerators engaged each community's recognised ruler directly, supplemented by resident interviews.
  4. Fixed categories, not free textResponses were recorded against set options wherever possible, so findings aggregate consistently regardless of who ran the interview.
  5. A named contact left behindEvery record carries a designated Blue Lagos community contact — a channel back, not a one-time data point.

Part 03/Water & sanitationBaseline report §3.4, §3.12

One exposure pathway, not two problems

Open or open-water defecation is the primary household toilet method in 81.3% of communities, rising to 94.7% in Lagos East. In the same settlements, a well is the everyday drinking water source for 61.9%, and river water — the least protected source there is — still serves 6.7% directly.

Read together with multi-day flood stagnation in the rainy season, water and sanitation here are not two line items. They are one shared exposure pathway, and should be budgeted as a single WASH programme.

Voice-only coverage is almost entirely a Lagos East problem — 74.7% of its communities against near-zero in Lagos West and Central. It blocks mobile banking, e-governance, and remote PVC and NIN self-service, which means several other gaps in this deck trace back to it.

Electricity runs the other way: the deepest gap is in Lagos West at 42.9%, where it compounds most strongly with health access.

Wood plus water. 56.7% wood-walled housing sits on top of the erosion and flood exposure in the register. Structural resilience and disaster planning should be read as one budget line, not two.

Part 03/LivelihoodsBaseline report §3.7, §3.11

Constrained by infrastructure, not by the livelihood

Fishing is the dominant occupation at 64.9%, then farming at 17.2% and trading at 12.7%. A community that lives on fish and harvest but cannot physically get either to market at a fair price is not economically weak — it is infrastructurally blocked.

0%50%100% of communities
17.2% skipping meals is a direct, measurable food-insecurity indicator and belongs in a welfare line of its own — separate from the longer-run market-access work.

Half of these communities have no active Esusu or Ajo — the cooperative and thrift structures they already understand. Where the tradition exists, supporting it is the cheapest available channel for micro-lending and economic resilience; it needs formalising, not inventing.

Disaster preparedness inverts the usual pattern: it is near-universal absence in Lagos West (98.2%) and Lagos Central (100%), the districts that score better on nearly everything else.

14.9% describe a past project as abandoned. That is a trust problem any new intervention has to visibly avoid repeating — which is what the named ongoing community contact in every record is for.

Where a security incident or land dispute arises, 86.6% go first to a traditional authority; police are the primary channel in 12.7%. That is not a gap to close — it is an existing governance structure, and the entry point every programme should be introduced through.

Three horizons

0–6 months

Immediate

  • Mobile health and immunisation outreach to the highest-severity communities, starting with Cluster 4
  • Mobile PVC and NIN registration drives run inside communities, sequenced by LGA registration rate, Lagos West first
  • Disaster committees and evacuation points established in Lagos West and Lagos Central, and in erosion-affected communities
6–18 months

Medium term

  • Motorised boat ambulances for Epe and Ibeju-Lekki, where unmotorised evacuation is most concentrated
  • 4G network extension to the Cluster 1 corridor in Lagos East — the highest-leverage single investment in the report
  • Electricity access by grid extension or solar mini-grid, prioritising Cluster 2 in Lagos West
18 months +

Long term

  • School access programme — secondary construction, transport or safe pedestrian bridges, targeting Cluster 3
  • Formalised sanitation — shared or flush facilities and managed waste collection, cross-referenced to the LGA tables
  • Cooperative and thrift formation, building on the Esusu and Ajo traditions already present
And one framing ask. That this baseline, and every intervention that follows it, is treated as direct input to Lagos State's 2052 mega-city plan — so that hard-to-reach riverine communities are built into that master plan rather than left outside it.

Funded against indicators, not against general need

Because every recommendation is anchored to a measured indicator, progress can be tracked by re-measuring the same 134-community register on a fixed cycle. No new evaluation framework, no new baseline survey, no gap between what was promised and what can be checked.

The monitoring set is the survey. Same register, same questions, same named contacts — which is also what makes it cheap to repeat.

Part 05/ProposalFor discussion
Proposal · not yet funded

KOH meets Lagos Riverine Communities

A proposed joint programme taking KOH's delivery model into the settlements in this register — the places where 91% have never had a programme of any kind, and where the barrier is almost always physical reach rather than willingness.

Blue Lagos supplies the half that is hardest to buy: the register, the named contact in each community, the recognised ruler who grants access, and the boats and field teams that get there. KOH supplies the intervention. Neither side has to build the other's half from scratch.

Why these communities specifically. A programme landing here is not competing with existing provision — in nine out of ten of these settlements there is none. The measured effect of a first intervention is therefore unusually large and unusually easy to attribute.
StatusProposal for discussion with KOH · scope and scale to be agreed jointly
Part 05/ProposalKOH meets Lagos Riverine Communities

How it would run

Phase 1 · Site selection

Pick the communities from evidence, in the room

Filter the live register against whatever KOH's programme is designed to change — health access, water, energy, connectivity, schooling — and take the ranked list straight off the platform. Severity score, population, cluster and access route are already attached to every community.

Output: a named shortlist with LGA, ruler, contact and coordinates.

Phase 2 · Pilot

A small number of communities, delivered properly

Introduce the programme through the recognised community head, which is how 86.6% of these communities already resolve anything that matters. Blue Lagos runs access, logistics and the community side; KOH runs the intervention itself.

Output: delivery in a defined set of communities, with a named contact retained in each.

Phase 3 · Measure and extend

Re-measure the same indicator on the same register

Because the baseline already exists for all 134 communities, the pilot's effect is measured against the same question asked the same way — not against a control group assembled after the fact. Extension is then sequenced by cluster.

Output: a before-and-after on a published indicator, per community.

Blue Lagos brings

The 134-community register and live platform · named contacts and rulers in every community · field teams, boats and access logistics · enumeration capacity for the re-measure · the government relationships already in place.

To agree with KOH

The intervention itself and its unit economics · target communities and pilot size · funding envelope and timeline · KOH's own reporting and branding requirements. This slide is the frame for that conversation, not a settled scope.
